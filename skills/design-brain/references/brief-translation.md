# Brief translation: from the client's words to design constraints

Clients describe what they want in adjectives. "Modern." "Premium." "Friendly."
Each of those words is a request for a set of design moves, and the job of this
table is to make the translation explicit before Step 1 of the method, so the
moves get chosen on purpose rather than by habit.

Three rules from the corpus govern the whole table:

- Cap the attribute list at three or four words, and make each one exclude
  something. Pair each with its opposite so everyone can see what is being
  traded away. Throw out any adjective a competitor could also claim (#822
  Brand attributes; Aaker's five dimensions are sincerity, excitement,
  competence, sophistication and ruggedness, and the scale itself is disputed,
  so treat the words as shared vocabulary rather than measurement).
- Tone is four independent dials, not one word: formal or casual, serious or
  funny, respectful or irreverent, matter-of-fact or enthusiastic (#850). Set
  them per context. Error states are never playful (#675).
- Typeface associations are learned and local. The most-cited study (Shaikh,
  Chaparro and Fox, 2006, 561 participants) found serifs read as stable,
  practical, mature and formal; scripts as youthful, creative and casual;
  monospace as plain; and sans serifs as nothing in particular. It is a
  self-report survey and the corpus marks it `disputed` (#341). Use it as a
  starting judgement, then test the shortlist on the client's real headline.

## Words that constrain nothing

"Honest", "innovative", "passionate", "quality", "professional" on their own,
"user-friendly". Nobody picks the opposite, so the word rules nothing out
(#822 pitfall). When a brief leans on them, ask what the client would trade
away to get it, and write the trade down. "Professional, meaning we will not
use illustration or humour" is a constraint. "Professional" is not.

## How to use the table

1. Take the brief's adjectives. Cap them at four. Reject any a competitor
   could also claim.
2. For each one, copy the row's exclusions into the decision log before
   anything else. Exclusions are the useful half.
3. Where two rows conflict (premium and friendly, say), the conflict is the
   design problem. Resolve it on purpose and write the resolution down:
   "Premium in structure and type, friendly in tone and imagery."
4. Set the four tone dials for each context the artefact has: marketing,
   instruction, errors, legal (#850).
5. Only then start Step 1 of the method.
